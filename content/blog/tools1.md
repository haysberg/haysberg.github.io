# Flatpak

What I find most baffling about Flatpak, is that for once, Linux distros can finally share a [single software store](https://flathub.org). It's fast, and it makes it so much easier to run a ton of software. I usually install packages using the shell, but I have already installed Fedora Kinoite on multiple old people's computers as a Windows 10 upgrade, and they managed to install everything they needed through Flatpak.

> I can't say this enough, Flatpak has fixed the distro fragmentation problem on the hardest nut to crack, which is "How can a non tech-savvy person install the apps they most want ?", and as such they deserve their spot on the list.

Besides my CLI tools, I don't have a ton of graphical software to install, the current list is : Discord, Spotify, Signal, Telegram and... 🥁 Zed.

# Zed

The first real code editor I used in my programming courses (around 2016) was [Atom](https://atom-editor.cc/). At the time, I really enjoyed moving my editor panes around, the way it was so pretty compared to its competition — not counting JetBrains IDEs, as those are paid software — the automatic highlighting, the complete settings...

After two years of a loving relationship, however, I grew tired of it. It was getting slow, and the minimalistic UI was not giving me enough under my fingers. So I jumped off the Atom train and moved to what, at the time, stuck as closely as possible to the philosophy I described at the start of this article, [Visual Studio Code](https://code.visualstudio.com/).

At first, it was awesome, and this time I stuck to it for around 4 years. It was faster, with more features and a thriving plugin ecosystem to handle a ton of different tools. I found it better in every way.

But after some years, I started to notice it feeling less and less snappy, and I had to fight with quite a lot of plugins just to handle my everyday work. My growing concern about privacy, combined with not being able to sync my settings between instances when using VSCodium, meant I stayed Microsoft's hostage for around a year, during which my IDE was working, but not as well as I wished.

Fast forward to 2023, and I stumble upon a Rust-based, GPU-accelerated IDE that looks like VSCode and IntelliJ IDEA had a baby. Basically all I have ever wanted... I couldn't believe it !

> During research for this article, I found out Zed actually comes from Atom devs !

Turns out, the Zed IDE on paper was everything I had ever wanted, but it was in very early stages and just couldn't do the job properly. So I stayed on VSCode again, reluctantly, until I put my hands on Zed once again around a year and a half ago, and oh my god, there's been so much work on this project. Everything I need works. It's so snappy I literally couldn't believe it at first.

I can :
- completely disable AI features with a single click (I'm not against using AI code, I just don't want any of it inside my IDE)
- sync my config across multiple machines by logging in with my GitHub account
- install extensions with a single click when opening an unsupported file type (even though most already are supported)

And the UI is so... so efficient. Blocky but modern, with the right amount of contrast (I use the default light mode theme), buttons that feel like they belong where they are in the window... I just don't see what could make me drop this absolute gem.
